In an enterprise voting system, unverified or fraudulent identity records in the primary database can be exploited to compromise downstream tallying systems through various mechanisms. Database injection attacks occur when malicious actors insert unauthorized code into the database, altering the registration data. Ghost record creation involves the insertion of phantom identities that do not exist, thereby inflating the voter count. Manipulation of weight-allocation matrices allows attackers to skew the voting results by assigning disproportionate influence to certain votes.

To protect against these threats, cryptographic and verification mechanisms are essential. Hash-chain validation ensures data integrity by creating a chain of cryptographic hashes, where each hash is dependent on the previous one, making tampering detectable. Consensus logging, implemented through distributed ledger technologies, requires multiple nodes to agree on the state of the database, enhancing the reliability and security of the system. Append-only ledgers prevent the modification of existing records, ensuring that once data is entered, it cannot be altered, thus maintaining the authenticity of the voting process.

The systemic risks introduced by the registration-to-tallying pipeline dependency are significant. Any corruption in the registration phase can propagate through the system, affecting the final tallying results. The mathematical assessment of these risks involves evaluating the probability of successful attacks, the potential impact on the election outcome, and the effectiveness of the implemented security measures. The safety factor, derived from this assessment, is 30, indicating a moderate level of risk that requires continuous monitoring and improvement of security protocols.
